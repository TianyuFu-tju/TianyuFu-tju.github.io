Sharing Research from Afar: ASLO–SIL 2026

2026.05.17TIANYU FU

This year, I contributed to the 2026 ASLO–SIL Joint Meeting, held in Montreal from May 12 to 16, through a prerecorded presentation. Although I took part from afar, I was happy to share my research with a community interested in aquatic plants and the connections between different water environments.

My talk, “Global Ecological Roles and Model Networks of Submerged Aquatic Vegetation Using AI-Aided Analysis,” was part of the session “Bridging the Gap in Macrophyte Research Across Realms: From Ecological Concepts to Nature-Based Solutions in Marine and Fresh Waters.” The session provided a fitting setting for work that looks across lakes, rivers, wetlands, and coastal waters.

Connecting ecological knowledge and models

Submerged aquatic vegetation can influence water movement, sediment stability, nutrient cycling, and habitat conditions. These plants are studied across many aquatic environments, often by research communities with their own terminology and modeling traditions. I wanted to understand how much ecological knowledge those communities share, and how that knowledge is reflected in the models they develop.

The presentation brought together two related analyses: a synthesis of the ecological roles of submerged aquatic vegetation, and an examination of the connections among process-based vegetation growth models. AI-assisted literature prescreening was followed by manual screening and coding, keeping the interpretation grounded in the underlying studies.

In the literature we reviewed, several ecological roles recurred across aquatic environments, including slowing water flow, stabilizing sediments, contributing to nutrient and carbon cycling, and providing food and shelter for other organisms. Model development, however, was divided among relatively separate model families, with limited transfer across ecosystems.

For me, this raises useful questions about how researchers can share process knowledge, compare model assumptions, and assess whether a formulation developed in one setting can be useful in another. Such transfers need testing, with attention to the ecological and physical conditions of each system. I was pleased to bring these questions to a session explicitly concerned with connections across aquatic environments.

Sharing a talk from a distance

Preparing a recording encouraged me to think carefully about what an audience needed to follow the story: the research questions, the relationship between the two analyses, and the significance of the findings. With the video carrying the presentation into the room, clear explanations and readable figures felt especially important.

My warm thanks go to Sabine Hilt and Morgan Botrel for helping arrange and play the recorded presentation. Their support made it possible for my work to be included in the session, and I really appreciate the time and care they gave to it.

Looking ahead

I am glad to have contributed to this meeting and grateful to the people who helped make it happen. I hope the work will encourage further conversations about submerged aquatic vegetation and how we model it. I also hope there will be future opportunities to meet members of this community in person, hear more about their work, and continue learning from one another.
